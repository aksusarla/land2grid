Grid connectivity

What a load flow study actually tells you, and why most developers only see it after it's too late.

October 2026·5 min read·Rajib Roy Choudhary, Founder

A connectivity application gets filed, and months later it gets rejected or scaled down, because a load flow study the discom ran internally found that the substation could not actually absorb the capacity applied for. By the time that happens, the project has usually already spent real money assuming the connection would clear.

Published capacity and actual capacity are not the same number

Every state publishes some version of available capacity at its substations, updated on a schedule that rarely matches how fast that capacity is actually being allocated. A substation that shows headroom on a published table can have other applications ahead of it in the queue, each of which reduces what is actually left by the time a load flow study runs. The published number is a starting point for a conversation, not a number to file an application against.

A load flow study is the discom's own calculation of how power actually distributes across the network once a new connection is added, accounting for existing load, other pending applications, and the physical limits of the lines and transformers involved. It is not a formality that happens after approval. It is the test an application has to pass to get approved in the first place, and it is a test most developers never see run until it has already decided their outcome.

What running the numbers before filing actually buys you

Working through a load flow assessment before an application is filed does two things. First, it tells you honestly whether the substation you are targeting can take the load, rather than hoping the discom's internal study agrees with the published table. Second, and this is the part that gets missed, it tells you what the discom is likely to ask for in terms of augmentation, whether that is a transformer upgrade, additional bay capacity or evacuation line reinforcement, before you are negotiating those costs under time pressure with an application already sitting in the queue.

An application filed against a substation that was never going to clear does not just waste the filing fee. It burns months in a queue, and by the time the rejection comes back, the capacity that might have been available at a neighbouring substation may no longer be either.

Treating connectivity as an engineering problem, not a paperwork one

The teams that get connectivity approvals through cleanly are the ones that treat the application as the last step of a technical process, not the first. The paperwork, the CTU or STU filing format and the documentation the utility wants, is genuinely the easy part once the technical groundwork is done. Getting that groundwork right, confirmed bay capacity, a load flow assessment that matches how the discom will actually evaluate the application, and a realistic read on augmentation requirements, is what actually determines whether that paperwork goes anywhere.
