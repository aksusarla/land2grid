Land management

Land aggregation is where solar projects actually stall, not land identification.

September 2026·5 min read·Rajib Roy Choudhary, Founder

Ask a team why a land parcel took six months longer than planned, and the answer is rarely a bad site. It is usually that the site was never one parcel to begin with.

Most usable solar sites in India, outside a handful of large single-owner tracts, are stitched together from several adjoining holdings. That is the part of the job that gets underestimated. Identifying a parcel that looks right on paper, checking contiguity, slope and substation proximity, is a few weeks of desk work. Getting five or six landowners, each with a different relationship to the land and a different reason to hold out, to actually agree to the same terms on the same timeline is a different kind of work entirely.

Why one holdout changes the economics of the whole parcel

A ten acre requirement rarely exists as a single ten acre plot. It is four acres from one family, three from another, two from a third, and a sliver that belongs to someone who inherited it and has not decided what they want yet. Every one of those pieces has to close, not most of them. A project that is ninety percent aggregated is not ninety percent done. It is stuck, because the missing piece is usually the one holding the most leverage, and that leverage only grows as everyone around it can see the project taking shape.

This is why we treat aggregation as the primary risk on a land timeline, not a formality that follows site identification. The sequencing matters. Approach every landowner in a cluster at roughly the same time, agree terms before anyone has visibility into who else has already signed, and avoid closing the easy parcels first. An easy parcel that closes early just tells the hardest holdout exactly how much pressure they can apply later.

What actually gets a cluster to close

Price matters less than most first-time land buyers expect. What closes a cluster of landowners is consistency: the same explanation of what the project means for their land, the same structure offered to everyone in roughly the same timeframe, and a visible point of contact who does not disappear between the outreach conversation and the registration appointment. Landowners talk to each other. An inconsistent offer across a cluster, even an unintentional one, becomes the story that circulates, and it is a harder story to walk back than it is to avoid in the first place.

The other thing that closes a cluster is clearing title and encumbrance risk before anyone signs, not after. A dispute that surfaces mid-construction does not just cost the specific parcel it is attached to. It stalls the financing and connectivity timeline for the whole project, because lenders and transmission utilities both want a clean land position before they commit.

What this means for how a timeline should actually be built

If aggregation, not identification, is the real risk, a land timeline that budgets a few weeks for "land acquisition" as a single line item is budgeting for the wrong problem. The honest version of that timeline treats aggregation as its own workstream, with its own view of which landowner in the cluster is likely to be the hard one, and starts that workstream the moment site identification has a credible shortlist, not after it is finalised.

It is a less satisfying way to plan a project, because it means admitting the schedule depends on a negotiation that is not fully in your control yet. But it is the version of the schedule that survives contact with an actual cluster of landowners, which is more than most land timelines can say.
