Open access or group captive: the structuring choice most C&I teams get backwards.

November 2026·4 min read·Rajib Roy Choudhary, Founder

Most commercial and industrial consumers come to a solar decision already favouring "open access" or already favouring "group captive," usually because someone else in their industry chose one and they assumed the same structure would fit. It often does not, and the mismatch tends to surface only after a term sheet is already being negotiated.

The actual question is not which structure is better

Open access and group captive solve different problems, and the choice between them should follow from how a consumer's load actually behaves, not from which structure is more talked about in their sector. Open access suits a consumer who wants to buy power from a project without taking on any equity position in it. It is simpler to enter and exit, but it comes with regulatory exposure, cross-subsidy surcharges and other charges that vary by state and can change with policy, that a buyer has to be willing to absorb or pass through.

Group captive requires the consumer, or a group of consumers, to hold a minimum equity stake in the generating project and consume a minimum share of its output, and in exchange it typically carries a more favourable charge structure. That trade only makes sense for a consumer whose load is stable and large enough to justify taking an equity position in a power project they do not otherwise operate.

Where the mismatch actually shows up

The teams that get this backwards are usually consumers with genuinely variable load, seasonal manufacturing, a business with real month to month swings, who are drawn to group captive because the headline savings look better on a spreadsheet built around average consumption. Average consumption is not what a captive structure is tested against. It is tested against minimum consumption thresholds that have to be met in practice, and a consumer whose load dips below that threshold in a slow quarter ends up carrying a structure that was supposed to save them money but does not fit how their business actually runs.

The reverse mismatch happens too. A consumer with large, stable, predictable load sticks with open access because it is the simpler structure to set up, and ends up paying surcharges year over year that a captive structure, with its higher upfront commitment, would have recovered well within its payback period.

What actually settles the decision

The honest way to make this call is to map a consumer's actual load profile, not their average, against both structures before recommending either one, and to be explicit about what happens to the economics in their worst realistic quarter, not just their typical one. A structure that only works in a good year is not a structure, it is a bet. For a commercial or industrial consumer deciding between open access and group captive, that is the conversation that should happen before the term sheet, not after it.
